# 07. Tax fundamentals

US-centric tax basics for indie SaaS operators. This is operational guidance; talk to a CPA for your specific situation.

## The three tax obligations you have

As an indie founder, you owe three kinds of tax to the federal government:

1. **Income tax**: on the profit your business makes (and on your personal income)
2. **Self-employment tax**: 15.3% on net profit (covers Social Security and Medicare)
3. **State income tax**: depending on the state you live and operate in (Texas, Florida, Nevada, Wyoming, and a few others have no state income tax)

Plus possibly:
- Sales tax (see file 03)
- Use tax on out-of-state purchases (rarely enforced for SaaS)
- Property tax on business equipment (rare for digital businesses)

## Quarterly estimated tax

If you're a sole prop, single-member LLC, or S-corp owner, you owe estimated tax payments four times a year, not annually.

Due dates:
- Q1 (Jan-Mar income): April 15
- Q2 (Apr-May income): June 15 (yes, 2 months not 3)
- Q3 (Jun-Aug income): September 15
- Q4 (Sep-Dec income): January 15 of the following year

If you don't pay quarterly and just settle the annual tax bill in April, the IRS charges underpayment penalties. The penalty is usually 5-8% annualized on the unpaid amount. Not huge, but avoidable.

## How much to set aside

Rule of thumb for indie SaaS operators in the US:

- **Federal income tax**: 15-22% of net profit, depending on your tax bracket
- **Self-employment tax**: 15.3% of net profit (up to ~$168K for Social Security portion in 2026)
- **State income tax**: 0-13% depending on your state

Total: usually **25-35% of net profit** should be set aside for taxes.

Set up a separate savings account for tax reserves. Every time the business deposits revenue, immediately move 25-30% to the tax account. Then check in quarterly.

## Deductible business expenses

Things you can deduct (reduce your taxable income by their amount):

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Software subscriptions | Hosting, ESP, design tools, productivity SaaS |
| Professional services | CPA, lawyer, contractors, virtual assistants |
| Home office | If you have a dedicated workspace (square-footage method or simplified method) |
| Business travel | Conferences, client meetings, including airfare and lodging |
| Equipment | Computer, monitor, desk, chair (over $2,500 may need depreciation) |
| Education | Courses, books, training related to your business |
| Marketing | Ads, sponsorships, content creation |
| Internet and phone | Business-use portion |
| Health insurance | If self-employed, often deductible |
| Retirement contributions | Solo 401k, SEP-IRA contributions |

What's NOT deductible:

- Commuting from home to your home office (it's already there)
- Clothing (unless it's a uniform with a logo)
- Personal meals that you don't conduct business at
- Entertainment (most was eliminated as a deduction in 2018)
- Tax penalties and fines
- Political contributions

## Home office deduction

If you have a dedicated workspace at home, you can deduct:

**Simplified method:** $5 per square foot, up to 300 square feet. Maximum $1,500/year.

**Actual expense method:** percentage of total home expenses (rent/mortgage interest, utilities, insurance) equal to the percentage of square footage used for business. More work, often larger deduction.

The space must be used *exclusively* and *regularly* for business. A corner of your bedroom that's also your reading nook doesn't qualify.

## Mileage deduction

If you drive for business (client meetings, conferences, supply runs), you can deduct mileage.

**Standard mileage rate (2026):** approximately $0.67 per business mile. Verify current year rate.

Track in an app (MileIQ, Stride) or a notebook. Date, destination, business purpose, miles.

## Equipment depreciation

Equipment over $2,500 generally must be depreciated over 5-7 years rather than fully deducted in year one. Exception: Section 179 lets you fully deduct most equipment in the year of purchase up to a high annual limit.

For most indie SaaS, almost everything you buy is below the depreciation threshold or qualifies for Section 179.

## Retirement contributions

Hugely underused by indie founders. You can put money into a retirement account and deduct it from your taxable income.

Options:

| Account | 2026 limit (approx) | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Solo 401k (employee + employer) | $69,000 total | Sole props / single-member LLCs with significant profit |
| SEP-IRA | 25% of net earnings up to ~$69,000 | Simpler than Solo 401k, less flexibility |
| Traditional IRA | $7,000 | Universal, lower limit |
| Roth IRA | $7,000 (income limits apply) | If you expect higher tax rates later |

A solo 401k for a $100K-profit founder can defer $30,000+ of taxable income to retirement. That's real money saved annually.

## Year-end tax moves

October-December is the time for tax planning, not December 31.

Common moves:

1. **Accelerate expenses**: buy needed equipment, prepay annual subscriptions, pay year-end bonuses before December 31 to take the deduction this year.
2. **Defer income**: invoice on December 30 to receive payment in January, pushing the income to next year.
3. **Max retirement contributions**: solo 401k contributions are due by the business's tax filing deadline (March 15 or April 15 depending on entity).
4. **Charitable contributions**: if you itemize, donate before December 31.
5. **Pay Q4 estimated tax** by January 15 to avoid underpayment penalty.

## Quarterly checklist

Each quarter (around the 10th of January, April, June, September):

- [ ] Pull profit and loss from your accounting tool (or sum your business bank statements)
- [ ] Calculate estimated tax owed (25-30% of net profit)
- [ ] Compare to your tax reserve account
- [ ] If reserve is low, transfer more in now (don't wait until tax due date)
- [ ] Pay quarterly estimated tax via IRS Direct Pay (irs.gov/payments)
- [ ] If applicable, pay state quarterly tax via your state's online portal

## Common mistakes

### Mistake 1: not setting aside taxes

Spending business income before setting aside taxes is the most common indie founder mistake. April rolls around and you owe $15K with $2K in the bank.

Set aside 25-30% from every deposit. Make it automatic if your bank supports it.

### Mistake 2: mixing personal and business

Already covered in file 01. Tax repercussion: an audit can disallow deductions if expenses are mixed.

### Mistake 3: missing quarterly payments

Underpayment penalty is small enough to ignore for the first year. After that, the IRS may require automatic payment plans.

### Mistake 4: doing taxes yourself when you shouldn't

Sole prop with simple income: TurboTax Self-Employed or similar is fine.

Single-member LLC: still simple, TurboTax works.

S-corp election, multi-state operations, or significant revenue: hire a CPA. The CPA cost ($1,500-5,000/year) saves multiples of that in deductions you'd miss and structure recommendations.

### Mistake 5: claiming aggressive deductions you can't justify

"My car is a business expense because I drove to a meeting once" gets you in trouble in an audit. Deduct what's documented and defensible. Skip what's marginal.
